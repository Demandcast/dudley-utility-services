01 · Audience

Who we're talking to

What this section is: The audiences

Every piece gets aimed at a specific reader before it's written. These are the three kinds of people who read DUS material, in priority order. When a piece can only serve one of them well, it serves the first.

First

The utility company

The client: right-of-way managers, supervisors, and leads up through directors, vice presidents, and the president. They decide whether Dudley gets on a master service agreement and into the bid.

Second

The landowner

Checking whether the person who knocked on their door is legitimate. Plain language and visible credentials do that work.

Third

The prospective agent

Careers material speaks to them. What lands is specifics about pay, training, and how field staff are treated.

Most readers arrive referred by someone they already trust, checking whether this company is real. Substantiate rather than persuade. Show the work.

What the client is actually buying

Confidence that the right-of-way will be secured on schedule.

02 · Positioning

What we say

What this section is: The core claim

The one idea we plant in every reader's head: what you get from DUS is certainty. Everything else we write exists to back that sentence up. The list below is the evidence we lean on.

The claim

Creating certainty through strategic land solutions.

The utility is buying confidence, not paperwork.

Mission and claim are different things. “Delivering energy through land” is Dudley Land Company's mission statement, retired for DUS surfaces: not to be used unless this rule is changed (2026-08-17). “Creating certainty through strategic land solutions” is what DUS sells, and it works as a display line where a surface calls for the positioning statement.

What backs it up

  • 45+ years. Dudley Land Company since 1980, thousands of clients, thousands of landmen, a nationwide footprint. DUS runs on that same infrastructure: accounting, finance, HR, systems, recruiting.
  • Command of the process. Describing the work accurately, including where it gets difficult, is the strongest credibility signal available.
  • Privately owned, controlling its own pace. A fact clients want, and the reason the culture claim is possible.
  • Culture, and what it produces. Investment in pay, training, and cross-trained land managers. The client feels it as continuity instead of turnover.
  • Utility focused, surface driven. Five industries: electric transmission, electric distribution, fiber optics and broadband, gas utility, water utility.

04 · Register

How we sound

What this section is: The voice

The four traits that make copy sound like DUS instead of any land company. Every draft gets checked against them.

Precise, and human

Our clients are structured people who expect rigor, across every department Dudley touches: engineering, public outreach, land, real estate, regulatory, procurement. They also work with landowners every day, and they know a vendor who sounds like a person builds better landowner relationships.

Garrett's version. We're regimented, we're controlled, and we're not robots.

Established, never new

DUS is not a startup and never reads as one. This is Dudley Land Company's 45+ years applied to utility infrastructure, under the same ownership, the same systems, and the same people. Inherit rather than announce.

Attribute the history honestly. Dudley Land Company earned the 45 years and DUS runs on it. Writing as though DUS itself has been doing this since 1980 crosses from inheritance into a false claim.

Certain, never boastful

Dudley Land Company's standard carries straight over: confidence without the ego, a reputation earned through results rather than self-promotion, and aiming to be the most trusted voice in the room over the loudest. Replace every superlative with the specific thing that would have justified it.

Specific over comprehensive

One concrete detail beats a list of twenty-three services. “Landowner negotiation” says nothing; “we knock doors, meet with landowner associations, attend the open houses and the city council meetings” shows the work. Results carry it too: miles negotiated, voltages worked, agents mobilized. Cut the list, keep the detail.

05 · Quality gate

The three tests

What this section is: The three tests

Every piece of copy passes all three checks before it ships. They're the mechanical version of everything above: if a sentence fails one, it gets cut or rewritten.

01

Logo swap

Put a competitor's name on the sentence. If it still works, it's a slogan. Cut it or replace it.

02

Certainty

Does the reader come away understanding how DUS removes uncertainty on their project? Explaining the industry in general doesn't count. The subject is always how we work, not how the work works.

03

Mechanism

Every claim carries the how. “We reduce risk” is a claim. “We convert blanket easements to defined right-of-way before survey” is a mechanism.

06 · Hard rules

Never

What this section is: The hard rules

The lines DUS content never crosses, no matter who writes it or where it appears. Most exist because crossing one costs credibility with the exact people we're trying to win.

Never name a client, project, route, or location

Write “a 760-mile transmission project across four states.”

Never name or negatively characterize a competitor

No contrast that depends on one.

Never mention renewables

No solar, wind, battery storage, or energy-transition framing anywhere.

Never imply the company is new

No launches, no announcements, no arriving.

Never lead with condemnation

A capability we can state, mid-sentence, mid-list. Never a headline, never the front of anything, never prominent where landowners will read it. Same for eminent domain.

Never use land vocabulary utilities don't use

No corridor: write right-of-way. No buyer: write client or utility company. The gas industry is gas utility, never gas pipeline.

Never define industry basics

Clients know what an easement is. Defining it reads as talking down to people who do this work daily.

Never brag about coming in under budget

In this market that reads as a bad estimate. The standard is in budget and on schedule.

Never use oil and gas vocabulary

No upstream, midstream, minerals, or leases in that sense.

Never turn a client's contract requirement into a promise

Deadlines and service levels in an RFP are obligations imposed on us. They belong to the client's contract.

Banned words

best-in-industry · turnkey · world-class · cutting-edge · revolutionary · seamless · industry-leading · scaled to fit your needs · one-stop shop · leverage

07 · In practice

Before and after

What this section is: Before and after

The same message written the generic way, then the DUS way. The left side fails at least one of the three tests; the right side passes all three. This is everything above, in practice.

Announcing instead of inheriting
Before

“As we launch Dudley Utility Services, we're building a team ready to take on utility infrastructure projects nationwide.”

After

“Dudley Land Company has spent 45+ years building a national network of land professionals. Dudley Utility Services recruits its right-of-way agents out of it.”

Superlatives standing in for substance
Before

“Our industry-leading team delivers world-class right-of-way services with unmatched attention to detail.”

After

“Every parcel on your project carries its deed reference, tract number, acreage, and current status, so you can see where the right-of-way stands without asking.”

A claim with no mechanism
Before

“We keep your project on schedule.”

After

“We keep every parcel's file complete and ready for whatever path it takes, so one holdout doesn't push your in-service date.”

Values as sentiment
Before

“Safety is one of our core values and we're committed to it on every project.”

After

“Our agents show up with their credentials on and their hard hats on. Utilities ask for it more every year, and it's the first thing a landowner sizes up when someone walks onto their property.”
